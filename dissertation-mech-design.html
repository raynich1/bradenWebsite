Mechanical design

Designing fixtures for testing at room temperature would be inconsequential—all you would need is a pair of clevises compatible with the sample and some connection to the machine. The heating is what makes it an interesting design project. With standard tensile tests, the sample is long and uniform, conforming to the coil geometry, so that the sample can pass through the coils and be gripped on either side. The grips are kept cool while the induction system heats the center of the sample to its test temperature, inducing a thermal gradient. Compact tension samples, though, have more complex geometry, and induction heating does not play nice with complex geometry.

A standard tensile sample vs a compact tension sample in the induction coils.

Simple water cooled grips were attempted, which resulted in a thermal gradient between the far edge of the sample and the mounting holes. This made a representative analysis at temperature impossible, since the analysis procedure assumes uniform properties.

This was a predictable result considering there was no appreciable thermal insulation between the sample and clevis—the pins were tungsten carbide, which has a high thermal conductivity. It was clear that for the sample to be at a uniform temperature, the clevises themselves had to be hot. This created both an interesting design problem and a material selection problem. There had to be a thermal gradient somewhere besides the sample, acting as the transition between the hot zone and the cool zone. Two ideas that did not work were:

  • Thermally insulating pins. This was impractical because the geometry that would allow for thermal insulation would also be bad for stress levels on the pin.
  • An insulating clevis. This would have been ideal, but no material exists and was easily accessible that is both a thermal insulator and strong in tension (with a transverse hole!) at 1000 °C.

The insulator had to be further back in the load train. The only suitable material for the insulator would be a ceramic, meaning its most favorable loading condition would be compression. Ultimately the below design was created.

The other notable feature of the design were the copper radiation shields. As the clevis was being housed in a large metal enclosure, it would be wise to minimize radiative heat transfer—enough radiation could lessen the effectiveness of the ceramic sleeve.

Regarding materials, a zirconia ceramic was chosen for the insulator (the white cylinder in the picture) and a titanium-zirconium-molybdenum (TZM) refractory metal for the clevis. Both of these maintain incredible strength for their loading conditions at high temperature.
